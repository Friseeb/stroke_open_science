Forty-five seconds. Public money pays for most stroke research; openness decides how many times it pays off. Waste: Chalmers and Glasziou estimated that 85% of health research investment is lost, roughly half at each of three stages: design, publication, reporting (Lancet 2009; the derivation is in their 2016 BMJ blog). Reuse: of 336 Johnson and Johnson trials shared through the YODA platform, 265 (79%) produced secondary papers, 1,167 in all, 209 by outside teams, and the outside share keeps rising (BMJ 2026). Saved: openly shared brain-imaging data were used in 913 papers by 2017, by authors in 50 countries; collecting those data again would have cost 893 million to 1.71 billion dollars (Milham 2018). Lost: genes that the company Celera held under licence for up to two years saw 20 to 30% less follow-on research and product development than public-domain genes (Williams 2013). The cost of not sharing in our field: when Bath's group asked 391 vascular and cognitive trials for their data, they obtained it for 78 (20%), after a median 241 days (Scutt 2020). Funders already require it: the NIH data-sharing policy has been in force since January 2023. The 2014 Lancet series on value and waste opened with a paper whose authors include Malcolm Macleod and Rustam Al-Shahi Salman (Edinburgh) and Ulrich Dirnagl (Center for Stroke Research Berlin).
