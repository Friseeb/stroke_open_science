Open data, closed code: 12 of 17 methods published on ATLAS released no code (Liew 2022).

Fifty seconds, first person. I came to stroke from imaging and neuroscience. There, a lesion study is built from open parts at every step: segmentation (MRIcron, ITK-SNAP, LINDA, nnU-Net; DeepISLES, an ensemble of the ISLES 2022 winners, was validated on 1,685 external scans and neuroradiologists preferred its masks to manual ones in a Turing-like test), normalisation that respects the lesion (cost-function masking, enantiomorphic normalisation, the SPM Clinical Toolbox, ANTs), lesion-symptom mapping (VLSM since Bates 2003; NiiStat, SVR-LSM, LESYMAP), disconnection (lesion network mapping, BCBtoolkit, the Lesion Quantification Toolkit) and sharing (BIDS, OpenNeuro with 1,913 public datasets today, NeuroVault). Most are BSD, GPL or Apache licensed; a few need MATLAB. The datasets are real: ATLAS v2.0 (1,271 MRIs, 955 public; raw data under a data use agreement), ISLES 2022 (250 of 400 public), the Aphasia Recovery Cohort and SOOP on OpenNeuro under CC0. The caveat on the footer is the bridge to the talk: open data did not bring open code by itself. Then the turn: when I moved to clinical stroke, the trial data and code were not there. Not on the slide any more: MRIcron, LINDA, DeepISLES, cost-function masking, enantiomorphic normalisation, NiiStat, SVR-LSM, the Lesion Quantification Toolkit, NeuroVault; the Aphasia Recovery Cohort (230 people with aphasia, 902 sessions, CC0) and SOOP (1,715 admissions with clinical MRI, CC0).
