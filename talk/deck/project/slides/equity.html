Off the slide: lower-middle-income authors publish 42% through routes that cost nothing (high income 19%), yet over 2015-2025 deposit or name public data in 1.8% (high 4.0%), mention GitHub in 0.3% (1.6%) and a trial registration in 5.7% (15.0%). The open-access route chart by income group was dropped from the slide. Income group known for 85% of the corpus: high 62%, upper-middle 32% (mostly China), lower-middle 6%, low 0.3% (400 articles). Seoul is a high-income audience with LMIC trialists in the room. Published open rises as income falls: 45% high, 61% upper-middle, 64% lower-middle (83% low, only 400 articles, do not lean on it); free to read by any route 67%, 70%, 75%. Upper-middle-income authors pay an APC for 43%. The data and code that lower-income teams could build on are not there, and their own work is the least likely to be shared: a data statement in 23% of lower-middle-income full texts (high 43%, upper-middle 57%), from only 609 audited articles. These full-text rates pool 2015-2025 over the audit's sample of up to 1,500 open-access full texts a year, so they average equal yearly samples, not the corpus. The gap holds in 2022-2025 alone: a statement in 37% of lower-middle vs 65% high and 78% upper-middle; deposit or public source 3.0% vs 6.2% and 8.7%. That is the inequality open science can close. Korea: 5,490 first-author articles, 64% published open (75% free to read), none with a data-repository link in the abstract or metadata; 1.3% of 1,293 audited full texts deposit or name a public source.
