NINDS 1995 · Dachs 2008 · Ingall 2004 · UK MHRA 2015 · NINDS archive, 15 Oct 2026

Sixty seconds. The trial this room knows best. Say as you click: the data were refused until 2003 even after a Freedom of Information petition; a regulator weighed the methods in 2015; the archive now stops taking requests. Takeaway, said not shown: the open data defended the trial, and showed what the paper could not. 1995: two parts, 624 patients; part 2 global OR 1.7 (1.2-2.6); symptomatic haemorrhage 6.4% vs 0.6%. Critics pointed to a baseline imbalance: more mild strokes in the tPA arm (NIHSS 0-5 in 42 vs 16 patients). Outside researchers asked for the data and were refused, even after a Freedom of Information petition to the FDA; meanwhile the trial's authors published at least 27 reports from the same dataset (Dachs 2008). After BMJ reporting in 2002, NINDS commissioned an independent committee, and in October 2003 the data went on sale: a CD-ROM for US$79 plus shipping, 100 variables, several SAS conversions and no key to the variables. The committee (Ingall 2004; none of its members had any connection with NINDS, the trial or the manufacturer; it first replicated the trial's tables from the data) analysed 622 of the 624 patients and found the benefit held after adjustment, OR 2.1 (1.5-2.9). It also found two things the papers had not said: no effect of time to treatment within 3 hours could be detected, and the blood-pressure records could not be analysed: 112 patients (18%) had identical admission and randomisation readings, 22 had none, and treatment after randomisation was not recorded. The committee said this 'seriously undermines' the claim that BP management contributed to the trial's success, yet still recommended rigorous BP management in t-PA protocols; Lindley, Wardlaw and Sandercock (2005) said the BP recommendations derived from NINDS needed reconsidering. Say it that way: the evidence for the BP rule was weaker than believed, not that BP control should stop. Then critics and defenders argued from the same file: Hoffman and Schriger 2009 (differences diminish on change in NIHSS), Mandava 2010 (benefit 13-23% smaller after matching, still significant), Saver 2010 (benefit confirmed). The UK regulator's expert group (2015) judged the adjusted analyses more appropriate than change-from-baseline, found that half of the 0-90 minute patients were treated at 89-90 minutes, so NINDS cannot test time-is-brain, and saw no regulatory implications. 2022-2023: a selection-bias claim and the investigators' reply that every randomisation error was disclosed to the FDA and most went against tPA. Today the NINDS archive page says data requests are no longer accepted; contact the Principal Investigators. In other words, available on request. The line to say: opening the data did not sink tPA; it defended it, and it found what the paper could not show. Do not present the critics' conclusions as settled.
