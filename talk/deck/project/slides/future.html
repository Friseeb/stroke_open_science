Say, do not show: the statement, retiring "on request" and promotion criteria cost nothing; Registered Reports, the prize and the toolkits need money, and most of the tools already exist. Full bullets: task force = a position statement (no stroke society has one); retire "available on request" in stroke journals in favour of a repository, a controlled-access platform, or a stated reason; a preregistration route for studies that are not trials (Registered Reports, or an analysis plan registered on OSF or ClinicalTrials.gov before the data are seen), with in-principle acceptance tied to WSO and ESO grants. Toolkits = imaging models and benchmarks tested in low- and middle-income settings; protocol and analysis templates, common data elements, consent that allows sharing; this benchmark for any journal, centre or country. Prize = the commitment index, verified in the full text, recomputed every six months, for institutions, journals, funders and countries, awarded at WSC; open-science items in promotion criteria (0 of 92 guidelines today). Targets in full: data in a repository or controlled-access platform for 25% of stroke articles by 2030; "on request" under 10%; registration for 10% of non-trial stroke studies by 2030 (1.5% of their abstracts in 2024), helped by a Registered Report or preregistration track in at least one stroke journal by 2028. Why not trials: they are already registered. ICMJE has required it since 2005, and 68% of open-access stroke trial reports (2022-2025) state a registration, most on ClinicalTrials.gov. The gap is everything else, 88% of what we publish: observational, imaging, lab and animal studies, where 7% of full texts and 1.5% of abstracts name one. And no stroke journal offers Registered Reports today (0 of 14 stroke and neurology journals), so the route has to be built. Two minutes. Three proposals, one per purpose. Task force, for reproducibility: a position statement (no stroke society has one), retiring "available on request" in Stroke, IJS, ESJ and JSCVD in favour of a repository, a controlled-access platform such as Vivli, or a stated reason, and a preregistration route for studies that are not trials (Registered Reports, or an analysis plan registered on OSF or ClinicalTrials.gov before the data are seen), with in-principle acceptance tied to WSO and ESO grants; embargoed sharing twelve months after publication can be the trial default. Toolkits, for access: WSO does not need to build them; most pieces exist (DeepISLES and nnU-Net for imaging, the NINDS stroke common data elements, published utility weights for the mRS, BIDS for imaging data). What is missing is stewardship: curating, validating, maintaining, and testing in low- and middle-income settings. Regulation is the real barrier for clinical imaging tools: under EU rules a hospital cannot hand its in-house tool to another hospital without a legal manufacturer, which a society-backed consortium could take on or broker. Consent matters too: the MR CLEAN Registry data cannot be shared because consent for sharing was never asked; IST-3 shows the alternative, its individual patient data open since 2021. Prize, for value: citations do not reward data sharing (no citation difference in our data), so the field has to, on the model of the Einstein Foundation Award, using the commitment index. Note that VISTA's rules exclude re-analysis of treatment effects, so it is not the model for open trial data. Invite the WSO leadership in the room to take the task force home. Say which items cost nothing and which need money.
