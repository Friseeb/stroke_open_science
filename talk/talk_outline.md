## 4. Reproducibility and replication (1:05, 45 s)

- [Figure: Same data and code reanalysed; independent participants assessed for the same question]
- Reproducibility
- Same data, same analysis.
- Replication
- New data, same question.
- Footer: Reference 14

Notes: Forty-five seconds. Use the National Academies' 2019 definitions. Reproducibility means obtaining consistent computational results with the original data, code, methods and analysis conditions. Replication asks whether a finding is consistent in a study collecting its own data to answer the same question. Different populations or interventions instead bear on generalizability. Transparency makes the material available for inspection; it does not itself establish successful reproduction or replication. The paired illustration is conceptual OpenAI-generated artwork, not measured findings. https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25303/chapter/2

## 5. SCORE: 55% of claims replicated (1:50, 60 s)

- Social and behavioural science
- Replicated
- Not replicated
- Footer: References 15, 16 · One mark per tested claim

Notes: Sixty seconds. This slide shows a single endpoint, replication, in one project. SCORE directly replicated 151 of 274 claims drawn from social and behavioural science papers published in 2009-2018 (55.1%; Nature 2026). Each mark is one assessed claim; red marks meet the project's primary replication criterion. Outcomes depend on that criterion and this selected sample. This is not a stroke replication rate, nor a before/after trend. The 2015 psychology project found 35 significant replications among 97 tested for significance; its different sample and endpoint cannot establish improvement over time. Statistical power, researcher surveys and data availability measure other constructs and are not pooled here. Tyner et al. doi:10.1038/s41586-025-10078-y.

## 6. Stroke research needs inspectable evidence (2:50, 50 s)

- [Figure: Stroke protocol, data and code available for independent inspection]
- Protocols explain what was planned.
- Data and code enable independent checks.
- Different patients can produce different results.
- Footer: References 14, 17

Notes: Fifty seconds. Now move from replication evidence in other disciplines to transparency in stroke. Animal-to-human translation is a generalizability problem. Trials enrolling different patients or using different interventions do not automatically test replication of the same claim. Tirofiban and subdural-haematoma trial contrasts have therefore been removed as evidence of a stroke reproducibility crisis. Access to protocols, data and code helps us investigate differences, with controlled access where patient privacy requires it. Missing public materials does not prove incorrect results. Our benchmark measures these enabling practices, not successful independent replication. Conceptual illustration generated with OpenAI Image Generation; not patient data.

Notes: Forty-five seconds. ## 17. A proposal for the WSO (10:55, 105 s)

- Create a WSO task force.
- Reward verified sharing.
- Track progress through this benchmark.

Notes: One minute forty-five. Proposal: ask WSO to convene a task force and develop a position statement; retire 'available on request' in Stroke, IJS, ESJ and JSCVD in favour of a repository, VISTA or Vivli, or a stated reason; Registered Reports for stroke trials with in-principle acceptance tied to WSO and ESO grants; embargoed sharing as the trial default, twelve months after publication. Prize: the commitment index, verified in full text and recomputed every six months, for institutions, journals, funders and countries. Awareness: the public benchmark; open-science items in promotion criteria (in 2020, 1 of 92 guidelines mentioned data sharing, and none open access or registration). Targets to discuss: data in a repository or controlled-access platform for 25% of stroke articles by 2030 (own repository deposit was 5.9% of 2025 open-access full texts); 'on request' under 10%; one Registered Report track in a stroke journal by 2028. The statement, retiring 'on request' and promotion criteria cost nothing; Registered Reports, the prize and the infrastructure need money, and the infrastructure mostly exists: VISTA and Vivli for trial data, ATLAS and OpenNeuro for imaging, ENIGMA for pooled analysis. Invite the WSO leadership in the room to take the task force home. Proposed targets and deadlines are discussion points, not commitments or current WSO policy.

## Corpus sensitivity (2026-10-13)

The benchmark corpus is an OpenAlex **title** search for the six stroke terms. A title-and-abstract search for
stroke 2024 (`--openalex-search-field title_and_abstract`, `scripts/compare_corpus_definitions.py`, table
`talk/outputs/tables/corpus_sensitivity_2024.csv`) returns 2.4 times as many in-corpus articles (37,878 vs 15,602;
99% of the title corpus is inside it) with the same open-access share (75.5% vs 75.2%; every status within 1.4
points), the same data, code and preprint rates, and a slightly lower registration signal (3.7% vs 4.3%, and
3.3% among the extra articles). The comparison script reads no abstracts, so these registration shares are of all
articles, not of those with an abstract: the title corpus's 4.3% is slide 11's 5.3%. The corpus definition does not
move the headline numbers; say so if asked.

Should we also search outside OpenAlex? We checked stroke 2024 against PubMed with the same six title terms (live
query, 13 October 2026). PubMed returns 11,816 records, and 94% of them are already in our corpus: 79% in 2024 and
most of the rest in 2023, because PubMed's 2024 search also catches articles that went online in 2023 and were
printed in 2024, while OpenAlex dates them by the online date. OpenAlex types most of what remains as letters,
errata, editorials, preprints, conference papers or book chapters, and it lacks only 7 PubMed records, all NCBI
Bookshelf entries. PubMed calls 384 of the records we do not count in any year research articles, reviews or case
reports; OpenAlex holds almost all of them, but our corpus rule or our title search leaves them out. Adding them
would move 2024 open access from 75.2% to 74.4%, the data, code, preprint and registration rates by about 0.1 point,
and the open-access status shares by under 1 point. The gap runs the other way: 37% of our 2024 corpus is not in
PubMed at all. That part is mostly regional journals outside MEDLINE and conference abstracts in journal
supplements, and it holds most of the diamond open access (2,259 of 3,051 diamond articles). So OpenAlex stays the
source, and PubMed serves as a check, not a second search.

If asked about PubMed-based audits: limited to the articles PubMed holds, our 2024 corpus would have the same open
access (75%) but a different mix: diamond 8% instead of 20%, gold over 40% instead of 30%, and a registration
identifier in 8% of abstracts instead of 5%. Open-access comparisons with PubMed-based studies are fair; status-mix
and registration comparisons depend on the source. The pipeline's own `--include-pubmed` option stays off (no
workflow passes it): as built it cannot add to the corpus, because PubMed rows carry no OpenAlex metadata, and it
can remove articles, because deduplication prefers the row with a PMID (3 corpus articles lost in the 2024 test).
Its date split also dropped 515 PMIDs (`return merged[:target_count]`, `scripts/search_stroke_dois.py:1682`).
